# "We should build a mobile app"

## 0. Before anything: that sentence is an answer, not a question

"Mobile app" is a delivery vehicle. Nobody wants one. People want a thing that the current product makes hard, and the phone is the shape they imagine the fix arriving in. If we generate solutions against "mobile app" we will build a mobile app — and the most likely outcome of that is a second, worse copy of the product that 6% of users open twice.

So the work below does two things: it reconstructs the problem the sentence is standing in for, then generates a wide set of candidates against *that* — including several that involve no app at all — and evaluates them side by side with the native app kept in the lineup as the baseline to beat.

---

## 1. The framing questions, and the answer I'm carrying

Since I can't ask, here is every question that would change the recommendation, with my working answer and how sensitive the conclusion is to it.

| # | Question | Assumption I'm carrying | If it's wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who said "we should build a mobile app"? | A senior stakeholder, not a user research finding. The phrase arrived as a conclusion. | If it came from a named cluster of user interviews, skip section 2 and go straight to candidate D or C. |
| 2 | Do we have an existing product? | Yes — a working web product used primarily at a desk. | If this is a net-new product with no web presence, most of this inverts: mobile-first is a legitimate starting posture and the analysis becomes a platform-choice question, not a surface-extension one. |
| 3 | What proportion of usage is already mobile? | Unknown — and this is the single most important unknown. I'll treat **weekly sessions from mobile browsers** as the decisive number. | See thresholds in section 5. |
| 4 | Are our users at desks? | Mixed: the primary persona is desk-bound; a secondary persona (approver, exec, field/on-site role) is not. | If the *primary* persona is genuinely non-desk, candidate D moves from "interesting" to "the actual product." |
| 5 | Is this a sales objection? | Partly. "Do you have a mobile app?" shows up in RFP checklists. | If we can name ≥3 lost deals where it was a stated reason, the value of a thin credible app rises sharply — but so does the case for the cheapest thing that passes the checkbox (candidate B). |
| 6 | Do we have mobile engineers? | No. Zero shipped native experience on the team. | If we have two experienced mobile engineers idle, native cost drops by roughly half and candidate C gets more attractive. |
| 7 | What would we stop doing? | Nothing has been named. This is a red flag. | A named trade ("we'd pause the reporting rebuild") means the org has actually decided; absence means it hasn't. |

**The honest state:** questions 3, 4 and 5 are answerable this week from data we already have, and until they are answered, any commitment to native is a guess wearing a roadmap.

## 2. The three problems that hide behind this sentence

The phrase almost always resolves to one of these. They look identical from the outside and have nothing in common underneath.

**(a) The credibility problem.** "We look unserious without one." The user is the buyer, not the end user. Success = a line item in an RFP and a store listing. The app barely needs to work.

**(b) The latency problem.** "Things stall when people are away from their desks." Approvals sit for two days. Alerts land in email and die there. The user is the *interrupting* persona — the approver, the manager, the on-call owner. Success = median time-to-decision drops.

**(c) The context problem.** "Our users are not at desks." The work genuinely happens standing up, in a warehouse, on a site, in a car. The phone isn't a convenience, it's the only available computer. Success = tasks that currently never get recorded start getting recorded.

Only (c) requires a native app. (b) is mostly solved by notification infrastructure plus a good mobile web surface. (a) is solved by the cheapest thing that installs.

**If you take one thing from this document:** find out which of a/b/c is true before scoping anything. They are separable by data we already have (session mix by device, approval latency distribution, role mix in the user table, CRM notes on lost deals).

## 3. Generation pass — 20 candidate moves

Generated by applying deliberate pressure from four directions. These are raw moves, not yet candidates; clustering follows.

### Shrink the surface until it's honest

1. **One screen, no keyboard.** A single "Today" view: read, and one-tap respond. No creation, no editing, no settings. Everything else deep-links to web. Forces the question "what are the three things someone actually does on a phone?"
2. **The two-week version.** Push notification + deep link into a mobile-responsive web page. Zero app, zero store review. If this doesn't move the latency metric, a native app won't either — it'd be the same content with a nicer shell.
3. **Make it the whole product.** If the entire company were only the mobile capture flow and the web app were demoted to back-office, what would we build? Worth asking even if we reject it — it exposes whether mobile is the frontier or the appendix.
4. **Deliberate friction.** Destructive and irreversible actions (deletes, billing, permissions, bulk edits) are web-only *by design*. Mobile is read-and-approve. The narrow surface becomes a safety feature we can put in the security questionnaire rather than a gap we apologize for.
5. **Right metaphor.** The metaphor is a triage queue you clear to zero — a pager, an inbox — not a shrunken dashboard. A table with horizontal scroll on a phone is the tell that nobody thought about this.

### Attack the premise

6. **Make the job unnecessary.** If the mobile need is "approve things faster," the strongest solution is policy-based auto-approval with rules and exception routing. Nobody approves anything on a phone because there is nothing left to approve. This kills the job instead of building UI for it.
7. **Reframe the request.** It is (a) credibility, (b) latency, or (c) context. See section 2. Three different products.
8. **Reverse the direction.** Instead of bringing the product to the phone, bring the phone to the product: the phone as a peripheral for the desktop session — scan a document into the open record, capture a signature, snap a photo, approve from the lock screen. The phone is an accessory, not a client.
9. **No constraints at all.** With unlimited budget: a background service that watches the account and pushes only the decisions a human must make, each with a drafted answer prefilled. The app is a decision inbox, not a product replica. Note that most of this value is server-side — the client is the cheap part.
10. **Unbundle one job.** Extract the single genuinely-mobile job (capture at point of work) into its own small, separately named app. Do not ship "our product, but on a phone."

### Fix what's actually broken

11. **The friction is auth, not screen size.** A large share of "we need an app" complaints are actually "I get logged out and SSO on mobile Safari is miserable." Persistent session, biometric unlock, and notification deep-links that land on the item — not the login screen.
12. **Name the anxiety.** The real away-from-desk feeling is "is something on fire and I don't know." Answer with a status surface and explicit confirmation receipts, not full CRUD.
13. **The unexpressed need: sharing.** People screenshot the product and paste it into Slack. Native share-sheet export of a clean, branded snapshot of any record is a small feature that matches behaviour people already have.
14. **One investment, three payoffs.** A real notification spine (events, preferences, delivery, digest logic) fixes email-deliverability complaints, powers in-app alerting, and gives growth a re-engagement lever — independent of whether a phone app ever ships. It is the prerequisite for every mobile candidate here.
15. **At 100x scale.** If this served every person in a customer's org — including people with no seat — the mobile surface is the free tier's front door. That changes pricing (seat model strains), auth (magic links, not SSO), and permissions (viewer roles). Worth knowing now, because it's expensive to retrofit.

### Make it spread

16. **Distribution built into the action.** Every mobile share sends a branded artifact to someone who may have no seat, with one-tap "open in <product>" that magic-links them into a read-only viewer. Mobile becomes an acquisition loop into accounts we already have.
17. **Arrive prepared.** Fifteen minutes before a calendar meeting, push a one-page brief on the account or project it relates to. Nobody asks for this; it's the reason they'd open the app daily.
18. **Make them look good.** A short digest that hands the user one number and one sentence they can repeat in a hallway. Users who look smart because of your product open it more.
19. **Combine with the calendar.** The home screen is the user's day, with product objects attached to the meetings that reference them. Fuses "what's next" with "what do I need to know."
20. **Bundle it as an offer.** Package notifications + approvals + status as an "on-the-go" capability in the higher tier. Tests willingness to pay before native cost is sunk — if nobody upgrades for it, that is the answer.

## 4. Clustered candidates

| | Candidate | What it is | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | **Notification spine** | Server-side events, preferences, delivery, digests. No client of any kind. Prerequisite for B/C/D. | 3–5 weeks |
| **B** | **Mobile web "Today" + installable PWA** | One responsive triage screen, biometric-friendly persistent session, deep links, add-to-home-screen. | 4–6 weeks after A |
| **C** | **Native decision inbox** | Thin native app: notify → read → one-tap decide → deep-link to web for anything else. Deliberately no parity. | 4–6 months + permanent tax |
| **D** | **Capture-first companion** | Separate small app for point-of-work capture: camera, voice, scan, offline queue. Web is the back office. | 5–7 months, needs offline sync |
| **E** | **Phone-as-peripheral** | No standalone app experience — the phone scans, signs, and confirms *for* the open desktop session. | 6–8 weeks |
| **F** | **Kill the job** | Rules engine + auto-approval + exception routing. Removes the need to act away from a desk. | 6–10 weeks |
| **G** | **Brief & share layer** | Pre-meeting briefs, weekly digest, share-sheet artifacts, viewer-seat magic links. Rides on A; lands on B or C. | 3–4 weeks after A |
| **Z** | **Full-parity native app** | The original ask. iOS + Android, most of the web feature set. | 12–18 months + permanent tax |

**The permanent tax on C, D and Z that rarely gets costed:** two more release trains, store review latency on every hotfix, a minimum-OS support policy that ages into a liability, crash/ANR triage as a new on-call surface, and a second copy of every permission and billing rule. Budget 15–25% of one engineer forever, per platform, starting the day you ship — not the day you start.

## 5. Evaluation

| | Valuable? | Usable? | Feasible? | Viable? | How to validate cheaply |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** Notification spine | High regardless of which problem is true | n/a (invisible) | High — familiar work | High: also fixes email complaints | Ship it; measure open/click by role |
| **B** Mobile web + PWA | High for (a) and (b) | Good if scoped to one screen | High | High — no store, no second train | Instrument mobile-web sessions *first*; painted-door the "install" prompt |
| **C** Native decision inbox | High only if (b) is true and B failed | Very good — best-in-class push and biometrics | Medium — no mobile skills in-house | Medium — permanent tax against a narrow surface | Only after B's numbers justify it |
| **D** Capture companion | Very high if (c) is true; near-zero otherwise | Good | Low-Medium — offline sync is the hard part, and it's genuinely hard | High if (c) is true — this is a new market segment | Five contextual interviews with non-desk users; a paper-and-camera concierge pilot for two weeks |
| **E** Phone-as-peripheral | Medium — narrow but real | Excellent when it fits | High | Medium — doesn't answer the RFP checkbox | Prototype the QR-handoff flow in a week |
| **F** Kill the job | Very high if (b) is true | Excellent — best UI is no UI | Medium — rule modelling is subtle | High — reduces support load too | Analyse the approval log: what share of approvals are rubber-stamps? If >70%, this beats every app here |
| **G** Brief & share | Medium-High; strongest retention lever in the set | Good | Medium — calendar integration is fiddly | Medium-High — feeds the viewer-seat loop | Send the brief by email first. If nobody opens the email, nobody opens the push |
| **Z** Full-parity app | Low — value is assumed, never measured | Poor — parity on a phone is a bad experience by construction | Low with current team | Low — largest cost, least differentiated | None available; it can only be validated by building it, which is the point against it |

### Classification

- **Differentiator:** D (if context problem is real), F (genuinely rare — most competitors build the app instead), G
- **Must-have to compete:** A — table stakes we're probably already behind on
- **Neutralizer:** B, C, Z — these close a gap competitors have closed; they don't create advantage
- **Quick win:** E

### Risk vs Impact

- **No-brainers (low risk, high impact):** **A**, **B**. Both are useful under every one of a/b/c. Neither forecloses anything.
- **Big bets (high risk, high impact):** **D**, **F**. Each can be decisive — D if the context problem is real, F if approvals are mostly rubber-stamps. Each deserves a two-week probe before commitment.
- **Quick wins (low risk, moderate value):** **E**, **G**.
- **Dud:** **Z**. Highest cost, weakest evidence, worst resulting experience, permanent carrying cost. If it ships, it ships as the accumulated end state of C — never as the starting scope.

### The thresholds that decide it

Set these now so the decision isn't re-litigated by whoever is loudest in the next planning meeting:

- **Mobile-browser sessions < 8% of weekly sessions** → native is a Dud. Do A + B and stop.
- **8–20%, and median approval latency > 24h** → the latency problem is real. Do A + B + F. Revisit C in one quarter on B's install and return-rate numbers.
- **> 20%, or a persona whose role is structurally non-desk** → the context problem is real. D becomes the serious conversation, and it is a *product* decision, not a channel decision.
- **≥ 3 deals lost in 12 months with "no mobile app" in the notes** → the credibility problem is real, and B's installable PWA with a store-adjacent listing is the cheapest honest answer. Verify the objection was decisive and not a polite proxy for price.

## 6. Recommendation

**Weeks 0–2 — settle which problem this is.** Pull four numbers: mobile-browser session share by role; the approval-latency distribution and the rubber-stamp rate; the count of users in structurally non-desk roles; and the CRM search for mobile-app objections in lost deals. Run five contextual interviews with the most mobile-looking segment. Nothing gets scoped until these land.

**Weeks 2–8 — build A, because it is right under every branch.** The notification spine is the prerequisite for every mobile candidate, it is useful with no client at all, and it is the part of "a mobile app" that is genuinely hard and genuinely ours. It also produces the telemetry that makes the next decision evidence-based instead of rhetorical.

**Weeks 8–14 — ship B, the one-screen mobile web surface, installable.** This is the cheapest thing that can satisfy the credibility problem, meaningfully dent the latency problem, and — most valuable of all — *measure real mobile demand with real users on real phones.* Install rate and 4-week return rate on B are the only honest forecast you will ever get for C.

**Then, and only then, branch** on the thresholds above.

**What I'd say out loud in the room:** we're not saying no to mobile. We're saying the first version of mobile is a notification spine and one screen, we'll know within a quarter whether a native app is worth 12 months of two engineers' lives, and we'll know it from install numbers rather than from anyone's conviction. If the numbers say yes, we'll have already built the half of the app that isn't the app.

**The thing most likely to be objected to:** "a PWA isn't a real app." True, and it doesn't need to be — it needs to answer which problem we have. If the objection is really "the store listing is the point," say so explicitly, because that's the credibility problem, and it has a much cheaper solution than the one being proposed.

## Stress Test

**Weakest part of this analysis.** It is built on assumed numbers. Every threshold in section 5 is defensible reasoning over invented inputs. If real mobile session share is 35%, the staged sequence looks like timidity dressed up as rigour, and "ship A and B first" costs two quarters of a real advantage. The framework survives; the pacing may not.

**Assumptions that could be wrong.**
- *That the existing web product is the centre of gravity.* If the market is shifting to mobile-primary in this category, incrementalism loses regardless of today's session mix. Current-state data cannot see a trend; it can only see a level.
- *That "no mobile engineers" is a hard constraint.* It's a hiring decision, not physics. If the strategic answer is D, hire for it rather than let staffing shape strategy — that's the tail wagging the dog and it's a very common way to get the wrong answer defensibly.
- *That the PWA install rate predicts native install rate.* It systematically under-predicts. App-store distribution, push permission prompts on iOS, and home-screen presence are not equivalent to an add-to-home-screen banner. Treat B's numbers as a floor, not an estimate — a weak PWA result is genuinely ambiguous, while a strong one is conclusive.
- *That the request is rational at all.* Sometimes "we should build a mobile app" means "I want us to look like a real company." That's a legitimate need with an illegitimate solution, and the right response is a conversation, not a roadmap.

**What a smart critic says.** "You've written a very well-reasoned case for doing the small thing. Categories get taken by whoever is native and excellent while the incumbent is running device-mix analyses. Your staged plan is optimal under the assumption that mobile is a feature — and if mobile is the platform shift in this category, it's the exact plan that loses." That critique is correct in one specific world: where the buying persona and the working persona are both migrating off the desktop. The four data pulls in Weeks 0–2 are chosen precisely to detect that world — and if they detect it, throw out the sequencing above and go straight to D with a hiring plan attached.

**What I still can't answer.** Whether the person who said this has information I don't — a lost deal, a customer conversation, a competitor's roadmap. Before running any of this, ask them what they saw. If the answer is specific, it beats the entire analysis above. If the answer is "everyone has one," the analysis stands.
